# Engineering inspectable AI agents for computational research with Galaxy

## Abstract

- AI agents can accelerate research, but trusting an answer requires seeing how it was produced.
- Through our interface, agents choose tools and parameters; Galaxy executes and records each job.
- On three benchmarks (3,780 runs), agents working in Galaxy scored close to agents writing custom code and every Galaxy run had a history link

## P1. Agents can speed up computational analyses but are not yet trusted; executing their plans in Galaxy makes each step inspectable

- AI agents now plan and execute computational biology analyses, from single questions to whole studies, and could make research faster, but researchers are adopting AI faster than they trust it. To execute a plan, an agent builds its environment at run time, installing software, choosing parameters and organizing files, and models make more mistakes as tasks get longer. A benchmark score shows whether an answer passed, not how it was produced.
- Galaxy, a workbench used worldwide, offers thousands of curated tools, compute for large analyses, and histories that record each job's tool, version, parameters, inputs and outputs. These features suit an agent executing a plan: it chooses among maintained tools instead of installing software, runs large jobs on Galaxy's compute, and leaves a record that a researcher can inspect. We engineered an MCP server and skills, separate from the community Galaxy-MCP, so that the agent selects tools and parameters and interprets outputs, while Galaxy executes and records the jobs.
- We compared four models working through Galaxy with the same models writing custom code, running each task three times in each condition, on BixBench-Verified-50, CompBioBench and ten multi-step analyses built from the peer-reviewed workflows of the Intergalactic Workflow Commission (IWC), in 3,780 runs (three models on the multi-step tasks). We report accuracy, the errors that prevented correct answers, and how answers varied between attempts. The results show where a workbench helps agents and what an agent's analysis needs before its answer can be trusted, moving agent evaluation from a single score toward analyses that can be inspected.

## P2. On BixBench and CompBioBench, agents working through Galaxy scored close to agents writing their own code

- On BixBench-Verified-50, 89.5% of Galaxy answers and 88.3% of custom-code answers were accepted (600 runs per condition; difference 1.2 points, 95% interval −1.5 to 4.2). Galaxy scored higher for three of four models, by 0.7 points for DeepSeek V4 Pro to 3.3 points for GPT-5.6 Sol, and 0.7 points lower for GPT-5.6 Luna. Observed BixBench scores varied more across model configurations than between conditions: Galaxy scores ranged from 83.3% for DeepSeek V4 Pro to 93.3% for GPT-5.5.
- On CompBioBench, the official scores were 87.00% for Galaxy and 86.75% for custom code (the mean of three replicate submissions per model, each scored out of 100), with no paired interval because official grades of individual answers are not available. Galaxy scored higher for GPT-5.6 Sol (92.3% against 91.0%) and DeepSeek V4 Pro (85.3% against 85.0%), the same for GPT-5.6 Luna (84.7%) and lower for GPT-5.5 (85.7% against 86.3%). These scores are in line with the 78% to 83% that CompBioBench's authors report for four agent harnesses.
- Observed scores were close on both benchmarks. These comparisons do not establish a general performance advantage for either condition, but they show that agents can answer these questions through Galaxy's tools and records at scores close to those of agents writing their own code.

## P3. On multi-step analyses, the strongest model passed most required results in both conditions, and the lower-scoring models passed more in Galaxy

- Each of the ten multi-step tasks asked agents to produce 8–12 required results from raw data. GPT-5.6 Sol passed 89.9% of required results in Galaxy and 88.0% with custom code (30 runs per condition), and passed every required result in 16 of 30 Galaxy runs and 18 of 30 custom-code runs.
- The two lower-scoring models passed more required results in Galaxy: GPT-5.6 Luna 49.5% against 33.9% with custom code, and DeepSeek V4 Pro 63.1% against 54.3%. For Luna the gain held without the imaging task described below (47.2% against 37.7%); for DeepSeek it came from that task (60.2% against 60.4% without it). Across the three models, Galaxy runs passed 67.5% of required results and custom-code runs 58.7% (8.8 points, 95% interval −6.1 to 27.1), and complete runs were rare in both conditions (24.4% of Galaxy runs and 23.3% of custom-code runs).
- On the multiplex tissue imaging task, the nine Galaxy runs (three per model) used tools installed in Galaxy, which also hosted the jobs and intermediate datasets; they took 6 to 21 hours and passed 70 of 81 required results. The nine custom-code runs passed 7 of 81 and stopped after 10 to 38 minutes, having substituted their own methods, in some runs because a package such as the segmentation model would not install. Galaxy supplied the pipeline's software, which the custom-code runs of all three models tried and failed to replace.

## P4. An error taxonomy shows which mistakes prevented correct answers, and how they arose

- Of the 133 wrong BixBench answers (63 Galaxy, 70 custom code), an audit attributes 84 to an under-specified task or its scoring rule (36 Galaxy, 48 custom code). We traced the other 49 in their run logs against the question and the accepted answer: 27 were knowledge errors, in which the agent chose the wrong analysis (12 Galaxy, 15 custom code); 18 were execution errors, in which the analysis was right but carrying it out went wrong (12 Galaxy, 6 custom code); and in 4 no agent mistake could be identified.
- Knowledge errors were mostly wrong parameters or thresholds (10), such as a non-default enrichment background, and wrong concepts (10), such as counting splice-region variants as coding variants or correlating with CRISPR gene-effect scores without inverting their sign; each changed the quantity computed, so the answer was wrong although every step ran. Execution errors were mostly data-handling slips (9), such as a filtered table that kept its header line so that the count was 19,160 instead of 19,159 (bix-52-q7), and tool versions that differ from the reference (5), such as BUSCO 5.7.1. These 14 slips happened in steps that ran to completion and returned a plausible value, so nothing in the run prompted a check; the other 4 execution errors were runs that ended without an answer (3) or accepted an intermediate result without checking it (1).
- None of the 18 execution errors was a request that the interface rejected. Rejected requests were common but absorbed: among successful runs with event logs, 87.6% of Galaxy runs (1,391 of 1,587) and 72.6% of custom-code runs (1,169 of 1,610) recorded at least one execution error, most often parameters the Galaxy interface rejected (55.6% of successful Galaxy runs) and software that was not available with custom code (46.9% of successful custom-code runs). On the multi-step tasks, 253 of 783 required results failed in Galaxy and 320 of 783 with custom code; the task pages name wrong parameters and misleading tool defaults as recurring causes in Galaxy, and unavailable software and substituted methods with custom code. In Galaxy, 7 of the 12 BixBench execution errors can be seen by opening the run's history, and recurring rejected requests give interface developers concrete problems to fix, with agents acting as virtual users.

## P5. Repeated attempts show that answers vary and that a correct attempt often exists, so results need validation

- Counting a question as passed when at least two of a model's three attempts passed (majority of three) changed scores by at most 2.2 points. On BixBench, it gave 90.0% for Galaxy and 90.5% for custom code, against 89.5% and 88.3% for single attempts; on CompBioBench, scored with the reconstructed answer key because official grades of individual answers are not available, 87.5% and 89.8%, against 86.6% and 88.3%; on the multi-step results, 69.0% and 58.1%, against 67.5% and 58.7%.
- At least one of three attempts passed (correct once) on 93.0% of Galaxy and 92.0% of custom-code BixBench model–question pairs, 92.5% and 96.0% on CompBioBench, and 86.5% and 70.9% of multi-step required results; 34 of the 49 wrong BixBench answers not attributed to the benchmark came from a model that answered correctly in another attempt in the same condition. Picking the right attempt needs validation: without the answer key, taking the answer given by at least two attempts accepted 82.5% of Galaxy and 80.0% of custom-code BixBench pairs, below single attempts, because 11.0% and 12.0% of pairs had no shared answer.
- The same model on the same question gave different outcomes in 15 of 200 Galaxy and 19 of 200 custom-code BixBench pairs, and in 51 of 400 Galaxy and 68 of 400 custom-code CompBioBench pairs. Fewer Galaxy pairs varied, consistent with curated tools fixing more of each analysis than code written anew in every attempt. On the multi-step results the pattern reversed (105 of 261 Galaxy and 63 of 261 custom-code results varied), because custom-code runs more often failed a result in all three attempts (74 against 34).

## P6. Token use was higher with Galaxy and lower in successive interface development batches

- In the main runs, Galaxy runs used 4.07 times as many tokens as custom-code runs on BixBench, 2.48 times on CompBioBench and 1.47 times on the multi-step analyses, and agents should be compared on cost as well as accuracy. On BixBench, token use did not signal whether a Galaxy answer was right: the median incorrect run used 1.85 million tokens and the median correct run 2.15 million, although a few incorrect runs used far more (mean 9.0 against 4.1 million).
- Development batches on BixBench followed the virtual-user cycle: examine the traces, revise the interface, measure again.
- Across batches, the observed Galaxy-to-custom-code token ratio was lowering as we better engineered the interface: with GPT-5.5, 5.48 (prompts and skills) and then 2.81 (MCP execution); with GPT-5.6 Sol, 3.45, then 2.42 (compact replies) and 1.53 (longer waits). Galaxy accuracy was held, the number of tasks accepted was of 136 to 139 of 150 answers correct. The main runs did not use the later changes, which were tested on BixBench only, and reducing this overhead matters so that the cost of Galaxy's features does not outweigh their benefits.

## P7. Galaxy kept scores close and helped the lower-scoring models on long analyses; the models, tasks and harness limit how far this generalizes

- Across three benchmarks, agents working through Galaxy scored close to agents writing their own code on BixBench and CompBioBench, and the lower-scoring models passed more multi-step results in Galaxy. The errors that prevented correct answers were mostly analytical choices and slips in steps that ran to completion, not rejected requests, and answers varied between attempts in both conditions, so each answer needs a check of how it was produced.
- The four models were run between July and October 2026 in one agent harness (three models on the multi-step tasks), and newer models have been released since, as the field moves fast. Because the strongest model scored high in both conditions and the lower-scoring models gained more from Galaxy, the pattern across models, rather than any one model's score, is what can guide how newer models are used with Galaxy.
- The tasks do not cover every method important to computational research: BixBench and CompBioBench ask single questions, and the ten multi-step tasks span ten analyses from genome assembly to tissue imaging. They are enough to show how agents use Galaxy's tools, where they fail and what their records reveal. Other limits: CompBioBench has no audit of failure causes; the error classifications await a second reader; we did not test whether people review answers better with histories; and 68 BixBench and CompBioBench runs with confirmed or possible exposure to earlier published answers were rerun under a stricter block (Methods), leaving the pooled official CompBioBench scores unchanged (87.00% and 86.75%).

## P8. Executed in Galaxy, an agent's analysis inherits the workbench's reproducibility, scale and curated tools, and stays open to human review

- Working inside Galaxy, an agent uses features built for human researchers: curated, versioned tools maintained by the community, including the IWC's peer-reviewed workflows as best practice; compute that scales to large analyses, such as the 6- to 21-hour imaging runs; access to software without installing it; and histories that record each job's tool, version, parameters, inputs and outputs, so that the analysis can be rerun. Its results come from tools the community maintains and tests, not from code written anew in each run.
- Galaxy's interface brings people into the evaluation. A researcher can open the agent's history, inspect each intermediate result, find mistakes such as the header line counted in bix-52-q7, rerun a step with revised parameters and validate the final answer. Every one of the 1,890 Galaxy runs has a history link, and histories can be shared and published with a link, so collaborators and reviewers see the same analysis.
- Pairing agents with Galaxy divides the work: the agent plans and chooses tools, Galaxy executes and records, and a person reviews. This moves agent-driven research from answers that must be taken on trust toward analyses that can be inspected, reproduced and shared.
